**REMARK**: Let $F$ be a [[Field|field]]. Then $\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[x]$ have several common properties.
1. They are commutative [[Ring|ring]]. (Go to [[Thm 22.2]])
2. They are [[Integral Domain|integral domain]]. (Go to [[Exercise 24|D[x] is integral domain]].)
3. They admit some division algorithm. (Go to [[Thm 23.1]])
4. All their [[Ideal|ideal]]s are principally generated. (Go to [[Thm 27.24]] and [[Principal ideal|principal ideal]].)
5. All their nonzero elements are uniquely factorized into product of primes (or irreducibles). (Go to [[Thm 23.20]])

___

In this section, we will define integral domains that satiesfy properties 4 and 5.
Then, we will prove that property 4 $\implies$ property 5.

Briefly speaking, let $R$ be an integral domain. Then
1. $R$ is called a **principal ideal domain ([[Principal ideal domain|PID]])** if every ideal of $R$ is principal.
2. $R$ is called a **unique factorization domain ([[Unique factorization domain|UFD]])** if every nonzero element can be uniquely factorized into product of irreducible elements.

For example, $\mathbb{Z}$ is both PID and UFD.
Also, if $F$ is a field, then $F[x]$ is both PID and UFD.

Our main purpose is
1. to prove that every PID is UFD. (Go to [[Thm 45.17]])
2. to prove that if $D$ is a UFD then $D[x]$ is a UFD. (Go to [[Thm 45.29]].)

This theorem provides a way to find out that a given integral domain $D$ is a UFD, through the structure of ideals of $D$.
